# Buying the Land

Everything else depends on this step. A cheap plot with a bad title, no access road, or in a flood zone becomes the most expensive mistake of the whole project.

## 1. Before You Pay Anything

- **Confirm the zoning** — Ask the Land Use and Planning Department of the local authority (municipal or district council) whether a residential unit is permitted on that plot. Some agricultural land cannot be built on without conversion.
- **Check for a building line / road reserve** — The RDA (Road Development Authority) may hold a reserve over part of the plot, which reduces your buildable area.
- **Check flood risk** — Walk the site after heavy rain if you can. Ask neighbours. See [[Architect]] — a flood-prone plot may need the house levelled up, which costs real money.
- **Check access** — Is there a legal right of way (*droit de passage*) to the plot, and is it registered? Verbal access agreements are worthless.
- **Check utilities are reachable** — How far is the nearest CEB pole and CWA main? Long connections are charged to you. See [[CEB]] and [[CWA]].

## 2. The Notary (Notaire)

In Mauritius, land transfer must go through a notary. **Choose your own notary — do not simply use the seller's.**

The notary will:
- Carry out a **title search** at the Registrar-General / Conservator of Mortgages to confirm the seller actually owns the land and that it is free of mortgages, charges, or *inscriptions*.
- Verify there is no **succession dispute** — inherited land with multiple heirs (*indivision*) is a common source of blocked sales. Every heir must consent.
- Draft the **deed of sale** (*acte de vente*) and register it.

Ask upfront for the notary's fees in writing. Notarial fees are regulated but there are also disbursements (registration duty, transcription fees, search fees).

## 3. Land Survey

Engage a **sworn land surveyor** (*arpenteur*) to:
- Produce a **survey plan** (*plan d'arpentage*) showing exact boundaries and area.
- Physically peg the boundaries on site.
- Confirm the plot on the ground matches the plot on the title — discrepancies between deed area and actual area are frequent.

You will need the survey plan for the [[permit]] application and for [[CWA]].

## 4. Morcellement / Excision

If you are buying a **portion** of a larger plot, the land must be legally divided first:
- **Excision** — carving out a small number of lots (commonly for family land). Applied for via a **BLP2** form. See [[permit]].
- **Morcellement** — larger subdivisions, requiring a Morcellement Permit from the Morcellement Board.

Do not pay for a portion of land that has not yet been legally excised. Get the timeline and who is responsible for the excision written into the agreement.

## 5. Soil Test

A **soil investigation / bearing capacity test** tells your structural engineer what foundation the ground can carry.

- Essential if you plan a first floor, or if the site is filled land, near a river, or on soft ground.
- Skipping it means your engineer must design conservatively (more expensive) or optimistically (dangerous).
- Cost: **[VERIFY]** — get current quotes from local geotechnical firms.

## 7. Checklist

- [ ] Zoning confirmed with local authority as residential
- [ ] Title search completed clean by your own notary
- [ ] No succession / *indivision* dispute
- [ ] Legal right of way confirmed and registered
- [ ] Survey plan obtained and boundaries pegged
- [ ] Flood risk assessed
- [ ] Distance to CEB and CWA connections known and costed
- [ ] Building line / road reserve checked with RDA
- [ ] Excision or morcellement complete (if buying a portion)
- [ ] Soil test done (if first floor planned or ground is questionable)
- [ ] Deed of sale registered and transcribed
- [ ] Applied for Home Ownership Refund Scheme if eligible — see [[MRA]]

## ⚠️ Important Note

🚨 **Never pay a deposit before your notary has confirmed the title is clean.** A signed *promesse de vente* with a deposit does not protect you if the seller cannot legally transfer the land.
